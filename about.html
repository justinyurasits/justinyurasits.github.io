—— About

Twelve years in enterprise product leadership. Several years inside construction operations. One problem that connected both.

I've spent my career building software products and working inside the operations they're meant to serve.

My background in enterprise product leadership gave me a way of thinking about how to turn operational complexity into tools people can use. Working inside construction operations showed me what actually needed to be built.

The work informed the tools. The tools are designed around the work.

The Turn

I didn't enter construction as an AI consultant.

My background is in enterprise product leadership. For more than a decade, I built and managed software products — working with technical teams, understanding user needs, and figuring out how to turn operational complexity into tools people could actually use.

Then I moved directly into construction operations. I wanted to understand how the work actually happened — not from the outside, but from inside it. I worked on projects. I learned how estimates get built, how documents get produced, how information moves between the office and the field, and where experienced judgment is the only thing keeping a process running.

That combination — product thinking applied from inside the operation — is where Construction OS came from.

What I found wasn't really a software problem.

The software existed. The problem was that the operation hadn't been made legible enough for it to work.

  • Valuable knowledge lived in experienced people — and left with them when they did.
  • Operating standards existed, but not in a form anyone could consistently use.
  • Repetitive production work consumed time that should have gone to judgment.
  • Most tools required the work to adapt to the software, not the other way around.
  • The business couldn't scale its best practices because they weren't written down anywhere useful.

The technology was ready. The operating knowledge wasn't in a form the technology could use.

You can't automate what the business hasn't made legible.

01

The first job is making the operation legible — capturing the knowledge, standards, and judgment that currently live in people's heads and putting it somewhere the work can use it.

02

Once the knowledge is legible, you can build systems that reflect how the business actually works — not how a generic software product assumes it should work.

03

Then automation follows structure. When the operation is well-defined, you can take the predictable work off the team and put people's attention where it actually matters.

That is the premise Construction OS is built on.

Construction OS grew out of solving real operating problems.

  • What if the business's best practices were built into the tool, not just remembered by senior people?
  • What if the right answer was available before someone had to track down the person who knew it?
  • What if production documents reflected company standards — not a blank template?
  • What if the team could see what needed attention across projects before it became a problem?
